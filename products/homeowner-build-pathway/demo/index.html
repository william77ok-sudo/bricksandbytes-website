Stage 5 of 20 · Design and permissions

Planning permission, permitted development and Lawful Development Certificates

Find out which planning route applies — permission, permitted development (often confirmed with an LDC), or listed building consent.

Likely relevant to this project

For this project

  • Provided by youYou said there is no planning permission or LDC yet.
  • Needs professional or authority confirmationYour designer can advise on the likely route, and your local planning authority can confirm it.
  • Provided by youYou described a side extension of two or more storeys.
  • Needs professional or authority confirmationPlanning Portal guidance says householder permitted development does not cover side extensions of more than one storey, so a planning application is likely to be needed. Only your local planning authority can confirm the route.

What it is for

Find out which planning route applies — permission, permitted development (often confirmed with an LDC), or listed building consent.

What to do next

Designer has advised on the likely planning route

Who to contact

  • Architect or architectural designer
  • Local planning authority

Essential checks

  • A confirmed planning position before construction contracts are signed.

Cautions

  • Assuming permitted development applies to a two-storey side extension.
  • Starting work before pre-commencement conditions are discharged.

In practice: Your designer normally prepares and submits any planning or LDC application for you, but the decision belongs to the council. Typical practice

What happens

  1. Your designer assesses which planning route is likely.
  2. If permission is needed, a householder application is submitted and the council consults neighbours.
  3. If the work may be permitted development, many homeowners apply for a Lawful Development Certificate for certainty.
  4. Any planning conditions are noted and discharged when required.

Why it matters

Building without the right planning position can lead to enforcement action and problems when selling.

Who is involved

  • Homeowner (the client)
  • Architect or architectural designer
  • Local planning authority

What to prepare

  • Drawings suitable for the application.
  • Application fee.
  • Any conservation or heritage information if relevant.

Decisions and documents

  • Planning permission decision, LDC, or written confirmation of the route.
  • List of planning conditions and when they must be met.

Questions to ask

  • Which planning route do you think applies, and why?
  • Would pre-application advice from the council be worthwhile?
  • Are there conditions that must be met before work starts?

Common mistakes

  • Assuming permitted development applies to a two-storey side extension.
  • Starting work before pre-commencement conditions are discharged.
  • Changing the design after approval without checking whether it needs fresh approval.

Resolve before moving on

  • A confirmed planning position before construction contracts are signed.

Timing

Most applications are decided within 8 weeks after validation; preparation adds time — project-dependent.

Often runs alongside: stages 6 (Party walls), 7 (Structure and technical design), 8 (Building Regulations). Technical design and Building Regulations work often runs alongside planning, at your risk if the design then changes.

Official guidance and sources

  • Official general guidanceGeneral ruleEngland

    Planning permission, Building Regulations approval and Party Wall matters are separate. You may need any combination of them, and having one does not mean you have the others.

    Source: Building regulations approval (GOV.UK · reviewed 18 October 2026)
    Party walls and building work (GOV.UK · reviewed 18 October 2026)

  • Official general guidanceGeneral ruleEngland

    Planning Portal guidance says householder permitted development does not cover side extensions of more than one storey, and that side extensions on designated land (such as conservation areas) need householder planning permission. Rules for other extensions have detailed limits and conditions.

    Who confirms this: Your local planning authority — only they can confirm the planning position for your property.

    Source: Extensions — Planning permission (householder permitted development guidance) (Planning Portal · reviewed 18 October 2026)

  • Official general guidanceGeneral ruleEngland

    Planning Portal guidance says a side extension can only be permitted development if it is a single storey, no more than four metres high and no more than half the width of the original house — and that on designated land all side extensions need householder planning permission. Other limits and conditions also apply.

    Who confirms this: Your local planning authority — only they can confirm the planning position for your property.

    Source: Extensions — Planning permission (householder permitted development guidance) (Planning Portal · reviewed 18 October 2026)

  • Official general guidanceGeneral ruleEngland

    Planning Portal guidance says an extension of more than one storey must not extend beyond the rear wall of the original house by more than three metres, or be within seven metres of any boundary opposite the rear wall, to be permitted development. On designated land, rear extensions of more than one storey need householder planning permission. Other limits and conditions also apply.

    Who confirms this: Your local planning authority.

    Source: Extensions — Planning permission (householder permitted development guidance) (Planning Portal · reviewed 18 October 2026)

  • Official general guidanceGeneral ruleEngland

    Planning Portal guidance says roof enlargements such as dormers can be permitted development for houses only within limits — including an added volume of no more than 40 cubic metres for terraced houses and 50 cubic metres for other houses, nothing beyond the existing roof slope on the main elevation facing a highway, and not on designated land. Other limits and conditions also apply.

    Who confirms this: Your local planning authority.

    Source: Loft conversion — Planning permission (householder permitted development guidance) (Planning Portal · reviewed 18 October 2026)

  • Official general guidanceGeneral ruleEngland

    Planning Portal guidance says the householder permitted development rules for extensions apply to houses only, not to flats or maisonettes.

    Who confirms this: Your local planning authority.

    Source: Extensions — Planning permission (householder permitted development guidance) (Planning Portal · reviewed 18 October 2026)

  • Official general guidanceGeneral ruleEngland

    Permitted development rights can be more limited or removed — for example on designated land such as conservation areas, or where the council has made an Article 4 direction.

    Who confirms this: Your local planning authority.

    Source: Extensions — Planning permission (householder permitted development guidance) (Planning Portal · reviewed 18 October 2026)

  • Official general guidanceGeneral ruleEngland

    A Lawful Development Certificate for proposed work is optional. It gives formal confirmation that the work as described would be lawful for planning purposes. It is not planning permission and does not replace Building Regulations.

    Source: Lawful development certificates (guidance) (GOV.UK · reviewed 18 October 2026)
    Lawful Development Certificate — consent types (Planning Portal · reviewed 18 October 2026)

  • Official general guidanceGeneral ruleEngland and Wales

    GOV.UK says most planning applications are decided within 8 weeks.

    Source: Planning permission — After you apply (decision timescales) (GOV.UK · reviewed 18 October 2026)

  • Official general guidanceGeneral ruleEngland

    Listed building consent is generally needed for demolition, alteration or extension that affects a listed building’s special architectural or historic interest, inside or outside. It is separate from planning permission, and carrying out such work without consent is a criminal offence.

    Who confirms this: Your local planning authority’s conservation officer.

    Source: Listed building consent — consent types (Planning Portal · reviewed 18 October 2026)

  • Official general guidanceGeneral ruleEngland

    Planning Portal guidance says an extension cannot be built forward of the principal elevation of the house — or, where it fronts a highway, the side elevation — under householder permitted development. Porches have their own separate allowance.

    Who confirms this: Your local planning authority — only they can confirm the planning position for your property.

    Source: Extensions — Planning permission (householder permitted development guidance) (Planning Portal · reviewed 18 October 2026)

  • Official general guidanceGeneral ruleEngland

    Planning Portal guidance says a porch can be permitted development for a house if its ground floor area (measured externally) is no more than three square metres, no part is more than three metres above ground level, and no part is within two metres of a boundary that fronts a highway. This does not apply to flats or maisonettes, or where a planning condition, Article 4 direction or other restriction removes these rights.

    Who confirms this: Your local planning authority.

    Source: Porches — Planning permission (householder permitted development guidance) (Planning Portal · reviewed 22 October 2026)

Plain-English terms

Permitted development
Some householder building work is allowed without a planning application, within strict limits and conditions. These rights can be removed or restricted for some homes and areas, so they never apply automatically to every house.
Lawful Development Certificate (LDC)
An optional certificate from your council confirming that proposed (or existing) work is lawful for planning purposes — for example, that it falls within permitted development. It is not planning permission and does not cover Building Regulations.
Householder planning application
The usual planning application for extensions and alterations to a single house. In most cases councils decide these within 8 weeks.
Article 4 direction
A council decision that removes some permitted development rights in a particular area, so a planning application may be needed where it otherwise would not be.
Designated land (Article 2(3) land)
Conservation areas, National Parks, National Landscapes (formerly AONBs), the Broads and World Heritage Sites. Permitted development rights are more limited here.
Listed building consent
Separate permission needed for most works that affect the character of a listed building, inside or out. Doing such work without consent can be a criminal offence.

Stage 6 of 20 · Design and permissions

Party Wall considerations

Check whether your work affects a shared wall, the boundary, or excavation near neighbours’ foundations, and serve notices in good time.

Likely relevant to this project

For this project

  • Provided by youYou said the property is semi-detached.
  • Needs professional or authority confirmationWork to a shared wall, or excavation near a neighbour’s foundations, may need notice under the Party Wall etc. Act. A party wall surveyor can confirm.

What it is for

Check whether your work affects a shared wall, the boundary, or excavation near neighbours’ foundations, and serve notices in good time.

What to do next

Identified any notifiable work

Who to contact

  • Party wall surveyor
  • Architect or architectural designer

Essential checks

  • Consents or awards in place before notifiable work starts.

Cautions

  • Serving notices too late.
  • Assuming planning permission covers party wall matters.

In practice: Talking to neighbours before formal notices arrive usually makes the process smoother. Party wall surveyors are often only needed if a neighbour does not consent. Typical practice

What happens

  1. You or your designer identify any notifiable work.
  2. Notices are served on the affected neighbours.
  3. Neighbours consent, or surveyors are appointed and an award is agreed.

Why it matters

Notice periods can delay the start of work if left late. Early, friendly contact with neighbours often helps.

Who is involved

  • Homeowner (the client)
  • Party wall surveyor
  • Architect or architectural designer

What to prepare

  • Drawings showing the work near the boundary.
  • Neighbours’ names and addresses (owners, not just occupiers).

Decisions and documents

  • Notices served.
  • Written consents or party wall award(s).

Questions to ask

  • Does any of our work fall under the Party Wall etc. Act?
  • When must notices be served so they do not delay our start date?
  • What will the surveyors’ fees likely be, and who normally pays?

Common mistakes

  • Serving notices too late.
  • Assuming planning permission covers party wall matters.

Resolve before moving on

  • Consents or awards in place before notifiable work starts.

Timing

Notice periods of one to two months, plus any award process — project-dependent.

Often runs alongside: stages 5 (Planning route), 7 (Structure and technical design), 8 (Building Regulations).

Official guidance and sources

  • Official general guidanceGeneral ruleEngland and Wales

    The Party Wall etc. Act 1996 covers work to an existing party wall or structure, building a new wall on or at the boundary, and some excavation near a neighbour’s building. Wooden fences are not party walls.

    Who confirms this: A party wall surveyor if you are unsure whether your work is covered.

    Source: Party walls and building work (GOV.UK · reviewed 18 October 2026)
    The Party Wall etc Act 1996: explanatory booklet (GOV.UK · reviewed 18 October 2026)

  • Official general guidanceGeneral ruleEngland and Wales

    The explanatory booklet describes notice periods of at least two months for work to an existing party wall, and at least one month for a new wall on the boundary line or excavation within 3 or 6 metres of a neighbour’s building (depending on depth). If a neighbour does not respond within 14 days, a dispute is generally treated as having arisen and surveyors are appointed.

    Who confirms this: A party wall surveyor.

    Source: The Party Wall etc Act 1996: explanatory booklet (GOV.UK · reviewed 18 October 2026)

  • Official general guidanceGeneral ruleEngland

    Planning permission, Building Regulations approval and Party Wall matters are separate. You may need any combination of them, and having one does not mean you have the others.

    Source: Building regulations approval (GOV.UK · reviewed 18 October 2026)
    Party walls and building work (GOV.UK · reviewed 18 October 2026)

Plain-English terms

Party wall
A wall shared with a neighbour, or on the boundary line. Some work to, near or below it needs formal notice under the Party Wall etc. Act 1996.
Party wall award
A document prepared by surveyor(s) when a neighbour does not consent, setting out what work can be done, how and when, and usually recording the neighbour’s property condition.

Stage 14 of 20 · Construction

Groundworks, foundations and drainage

Demolition where needed, excavation, foundations, drainage and the ground floor slab.

Likely relevant to this project

For this project

  • Typical practiceDemolition and opening-up often reveal unexpected conditions. Agree how changes will be priced before work starts.

What it is for

Demolition where needed, excavation, foundations, drainage and the ground floor slab.

What to do next

Foundations inspected

Who to contact

  • Main contractor (builder)
  • Individual trades
  • Building Control body
  • Structural engineer

Essential checks

  • Foundation and drainage inspections completed.

Cautions

  • Agreeing foundation changes verbally without a price.
  • Covering work before inspection.

In practice: Groundworks are where hidden conditions often show up. Be ready to make decisions quickly, but agree prices for changes before work continues. Typical practice

What happens

  1. Any demolition and opening-up is carried out safely.
  2. Foundations are dug, inspected and poured.
  3. Drainage is laid and tested.
  4. Walls are built up to damp-proof course level.

Why it matters

Unexpected ground or drainage conditions are among the most common causes of cost and time changes.

Who is involved

  • Main contractor (builder)
  • Individual trades
  • Building Control body
  • Structural engineer

What to prepare

  • Be ready to make quick decisions if ground conditions differ.
  • Know how changes will be priced.

Decisions and documents

  • Building Control inspections recorded.
  • Any changes agreed in writing.

Questions to ask

  • Has Building Control inspected the foundations before concrete?
  • Has drainage been tested?
  • Are any changes being priced before they are done?

Common mistakes

  • Agreeing foundation changes verbally without a price.
  • Covering work before inspection.

Resolve before moving on

  • Foundation and drainage inspections completed.

Timing

Often 2–6 weeks — project-dependent.

Often runs alongside: stages 13 (Site setup), 15 (Structure and shell).

Official guidance and sources

  • Typical practiceTypical practiceEngland

    Building Control usually inspects at key stages such as foundation excavations, drainage, damp-proof course, structural elements and completion. The exact stages and how much notice to give vary.

    Who confirms this: Your Building Control body — ask for its inspection schedule at the start.

    Source: The Building Regulations 2010, regulation 16 (notices and inspection of building work) (legislation.gov.uk · reviewed 22 October 2026)

  • Typical practiceDepends on your projectEngland

    If a public sewer runs under or near the extension, the water company normally needs to be consulted, and a build-over agreement may be required. Drainage is covered by Approved Document H.

    Who confirms this: Your water company and Building Control body.

    Source: Building regulations and approved documents index (GOV.UK · reviewed 18 October 2026)

  • Official general guidanceGeneral ruleEngland

    In England, the local authority may require notice at particular stages of the work, but only for stages it intends to inspect based on its own assessment of risk. There is no single fixed list for every project. It can also require work that was covered up without the notice it needed to be opened up so it can be checked. An inspection is a check, not an approval of the whole project.

    Who confirms this: Your building control body — ask for its inspection plan and notice periods before work starts.

    Source: The Building Regulations 2010, regulation 16 (notices and inspection of building work) (legislation.gov.uk · reviewed 22 October 2026)

Plain-English terms

Damp-proof course (DPC) and membrane (DPM)
Layers that stop moisture rising through walls (DPC) and floors (DPM).
Build-over agreement
Permission from the water company to build over or near a public sewer. Ask your water company early if drains run under or near the extension.
Temporary works / propping
Supports used to hold up walls or floors safely while structure is being altered — for example while a beam is installed.

Building control inspection check

  • Foundation excavations — usually checked before concrete is poured.
  • Below-ground drainage — usually checked before it is covered over.

Confirm with your building control body which stages it wants to inspect and how much notice it needs. It decides this based on the project, may ask for additional inspections, and an inspection is not an approval of the whole project.
